## The story of this chapter

The question of this chapter is: what number do the values $f(x)$ approach as $x$ approaches
$a$, and how can we be sure? A table of values can suggest an answer and still mislead.
[The Limit of a Function](#calc-limit) replaces "approach" by the ε–δ definition, a game in which
every tolerance $\eps$ must be met by some window $\delta$ around $a$, and in which the value
$f(a)$ plays no part. [One-Sided Limits](#calc-one-sided-limits) plays the same game on each side
of $a$ separately: the limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and agree, which
explains jumps. Proving every limit from the definition would be slow, so
[Limit Laws](#calc-limit-laws) proves once that limits respect sums, products, quotients, powers
and square roots; for polynomials and rational functions they give direct substitution.

The laws fail where a denominator approaches $0$. [Computing Limits
Algebraically](#calc-computing-limits) handles the form $\frac{0}{0}$: factor, rationalise or
simplify, and then a lemma says that functions which agree except at $a$ have the same limit.
Where no algebra helps, [The Squeeze Theorem](#calc-squeeze-theorem) traps a function between two
with the same limit; it tames oscillating factors and proves $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$
without derivatives. The last two pages let $\infty$ in, once as a value and once as a place.
[Infinite Limits and Vertical Asymptotes](#calc-infinite-limits) describes values that grow without
bound near a point, found by sign analysis, and shows that such a "limit" is not a real limit.
[Limits at Infinity and Horizontal Asymptotes](#calc-limits-at-infinity) describes the far ends of
a graph, with the same laws as before and the degrees of a rational function deciding the answer.
The exercises below mix all seven pages, so choosing the method is part of each task.

## Review exercises

::::{exercise} Substitute or simplify?
:label: exr-calc-limits-review-substitute-or-simplify
:class: tier-a

Let $p(x) = x^2 + 2x - 3$. Find
(a) $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{p(x)}{x^2 + 1}$ and
(b) $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{p(x)}{x^2 - 1}$.

:::{admonition} Hint 1
:class: dropdown hint
In which of the two quotients is the denominator $0$ at $x = 1$? Only there is simplifying needed.
:::

:::{admonition} Answer
:class: dropdown answer
(a) $0$ (b) $2$
:::
::::

::::{solution} exr-calc-limits-review-substitute-or-simplify
:label: sol-calc-limits-review-substitute-or-simplify
:class: dropdown

**(a)** The denominator $q(x) = x^2 + 1$ has $q(1) = 2 \ne 0$, so by [part (b) of direct
substitution](#cor-calc-direct-substitution) the limit is
$$
\frac{p(1)}{q(1)} = \frac{1 + 2 - 3}{2} = 0 .
$$
A numerator that approaches $0$ does no harm while the denominator does not.

**(b)** Here the denominator is $1 - 1 = 0$ at $x = 1$, so neither direct substitution nor the
quotient law, [part (d) of the limit laws](#thm-calc-limit-laws), applies. The numerator is $0$
at $1$ too. Multiplying out shows that
$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + 2x - 3 &= (x - 1)(x + 3), \\
x^2 - 1 &= (x - 1)(x + 1).
\end{aligned}
$$
Let $0 < \abs{x - 1} < 2$. By [part (e) of the proposition on distance
inequalities](#prop-calc-abs-interval), $-1 < x < 1$ or $1 < x < 3$, so $x \ne 1$ and
$x \ne -1$. Then $x - 1 \ne 0$ and $x + 1 \ne 0$, the quotient is defined, and cancelling the
non-zero factor $x - 1$ gives
$$
\frac{x^2 + 2x - 3}{x^2 - 1} = \frac{x + 3}{x + 1} .
$$
The right-hand side is a rational function whose denominator is $2 \ne 0$ at $1$, so by part (b)
of direct substitution its limit at $1$ is $\frac{4}{2} = 2$. The two functions are defined and
equal at every $x$ with $0 < \abs{x - 1} < 2$, so by [the lemma on functions that agree except
at a point](#lem-calc-limit-agree-except-point), with $r = 2$, the limit in (b) is $2$ as well.

::::{exercise} Sine over a quadratic
:label: exr-calc-limits-review-sine-over-quadratic
:class: tier-a

Find $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x^2 + 3x}$.

:::{admonition} Hint 1
:class: dropdown hint
Factor the denominator, and look for $\frac{\sin x}{x}$.
:::

:::{admonition} Answer
:class: dropdown answer
$\frac{1}{3}$
:::
::::

::::{solution} exr-calc-limits-review-sine-over-quadratic
:label: sol-calc-limits-review-sine-over-quadratic
:class: dropdown

The denominator is $x^2 + 3x = x(x + 3)$, which is $0$ exactly when $x = 0$ or $x = -3$, by
[part (a) of the sign rules](#prop-calc-sign-rules). So the function is defined at every
$x \ne 0, -3$, and for every such $x$
$$
\frac{\sin x}{x^2 + 3x} = \frac{\sin x}{x} \cdot \frac{1}{x + 3} .
$$
The right-hand side is defined at exactly the same $x$, so the two sides are the same function.
Both factors are defined at every $x$ with $0 < \abs{x} < 3$. By [the theorem on the limit of
$\frac{\sin x}{x}$](#thm-calc-sin-x-over-x), the first factor approaches $1$. The second is a
rational function whose denominator is $3 \ne 0$ at $0$, so by [part (b) of direct
substitution](#cor-calc-direct-substitution) it approaches $\frac{1}{3}$. By the product law,
[part (c) of the limit laws](#thm-calc-limit-laws),
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x^2 + 3x} = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{3} .
$$

::::{exercise} An absolute value below
:label: exr-calc-limits-review-abs-quotient
:class: tier-a

Let $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 4}{\abs{x - 2}}$ for $x \ne 2$. Find
(a) $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2^{-}} f(x)$ and (b) $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2^{+}} f(x)$,
and show that $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$ does not exist.

:::{admonition} Hint 1
:class: dropdown hint
What is $\abs{x - 2}$ for $x < 2$, and for $x > 2$? Simplify $f(x)$ on each side.
:::

:::{admonition} Answer
:class: dropdown answer
(a) ${-4}$ (b) $4$
:::
::::

::::{solution} exr-calc-limits-review-abs-quotient
:label: sol-calc-limits-review-abs-quotient
:class: dropdown

**On each side.** For every $x$, $x^2 - 4 = (x - 2)(x + 2)$. By [part (b) of the sign
rules](#prop-calc-sign-rules), $x - 2 > 0$ for $x > 2$ and $x - 2 < 0$ for $x < 2$, so by
[the definition of the absolute value](#def-calc-absolute-value), $\abs{x - 2} = x - 2$ for
$x > 2$ and $\abs{x - 2} = -(x - 2)$ for $x < 2$. Cancelling the non-zero number $x - 2$, $f(x) = x + 2$ for $x > 2$, and $f(x) = -(x + 2)$ for $x < 2$.

**The one-sided limits of the formulas.** The polynomials $x + 2$ and $-(x + 2)$ have the limits
$4$ and ${-4}$ at $2$, by [part (a) of direct substitution](#cor-calc-direct-substitution). They are
defined at every real number, so by [the theorem on a limit and its one-sided
limits](#thm-calc-limit-iff-one-sided), $\lim_{x \to 2^{+}} (x + 2) = 4$ and
$\lim_{x \to 2^{-}} \bigl(-(x + 2)\bigr) = -4$.

**From the formulas to $f$.** Let $\eps > 0$. By [part (a) of the definition of one-sided
limits](#def-calc-one-sided-limit), some $\delta > 0$ has $\abs{(x + 2) - 4} < \eps$ whenever
$2 < x < 2 + \delta$. Every such $x$ has $x > 2$, so $f(x)$ is defined and equals $x + 2$, and
$\abs{f(x) - 4} < \eps$. So the same $\delta$ wins the round $\eps$ for $f$, and
$\lim_{x \to 2^{+}} f(x) = 4$. In the same way, with part (b) of that definition and the
half-window $2 - \delta < x < 2$, where $f(x) = -(x + 2)$, $\lim_{x \to 2^{-}} f(x) = -4$.

**The limit.** $f$ is defined at every point of the open interval $(1, 3)$ except $2$, so it is
as in [the definition of the limit](#def-calc-limit). Its one-sided limits at $2$ are ${-4}$ and
$4$, which differ, so $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$ does not exist, by [part (a) of the corollary on
one-sided limits that differ](#cor-calc-one-sided-limits-differ). By [the remark on the
jump](#rem-calc-one-sided-limits-jump), $f$ jumps by $4 - (-4) = 8$ at $2$.

::::{exercise} Which lines are asymptotes?
:label: exr-calc-limits-review-asymptotes
:class: tier-b

Let $f(x) = \dfrac{2x^2 + 2x}{x^2 - 2x - 3}$. Find (a) every zero $a$ of the denominator for which
the line $x = a$ is a vertical asymptote of the graph of $f$, and (b) every number $L$ for which
the line $y = L$ is a horizontal asymptote of the graph of $f$.

:::{admonition} Hint 1
:class: dropdown hint
Factor the numerator and the denominator. One zero of the denominator is a zero of the numerator
too: what happens there?
:::

:::{admonition} Hint 2
:class: dropdown hint
For the horizontal asymptotes, compare the degrees of the numerator and the denominator, as in
[the proposition on rational functions at infinity](#prop-calc-rational-at-infinity).
:::

:::{admonition} Answer
:class: dropdown answer
(a) $3$ (b) $2$
:::
::::

::::{solution} exr-calc-limits-review-asymptotes
:label: sol-calc-limits-review-asymptotes
:class: dropdown

**Factor.** $2x^2 + 2x = 2x(x + 1)$ and $x^2 - 2x - 3 = (x + 1)(x - 3)$. The denominator is $0$
exactly at $x = -1$ and $x = 3$, by [part (a) of the sign rules](#prop-calc-sign-rules). At every
$x \ne -1, 3$ we may cancel the non-zero factor $x + 1$:
$$
f(x) = \frac{2x}{x - 3} .
$$

**At $3$: an asymptote.** Let $3 < x < 4$. Then $x \ne -1$, so $f(x) = 2x \cdot \frac{1}{x - 3}$.
Multiplying $x > 3$ by the positive number $2$ keeps it ([property 5(a) of the order
rules](#rem-calc-order-rules)), so $2x > 6$. By [part (a) of the proposition on reciprocal
powers](#prop-calc-reciprocal-power-limits), with $a = 3$ and $n = 1$,
$\lim_{x \to 3^{+}} \frac{1}{x - 3} = \infty$. By [part (a) of the proposition on a factor that
stays away from $0$](#prop-calc-infinite-limit-product), with $h(x) = 2x \ge 6 = m$ on $(3, 4)$,
$\lim_{x \to 3^{+}} f(x) = \infty$. So the line $x = 3$ is a vertical asymptote, by [the
definition of a vertical asymptote](#def-calc-vertical-asymptote).

**At ${-1}$: a hole.** Let $0 < \abs{x + 1} < 1$. By [part (e) of the proposition on distance
inequalities](#prop-calc-abs-interval), $-2 < x < -1$ or $-1 < x < 0$, so $x \ne 3$, $f(x)$ is
defined, and $f(x) = \frac{2x}{x - 3}$. This rational function has the denominator ${-4} \ne 0$
at ${-1}$, so by [part (b) of direct substitution](#cor-calc-direct-substitution) its limit at
${-1}$ is $\frac{-2}{-4} = \frac{1}{2}$. By [the lemma on functions that agree except at a
point](#lem-calc-limit-agree-except-point), with $r = 1$, $\lim_{x \to -1} f(x) = \frac12$. Then,
by [the theorem on a limit and its one-sided limits](#thm-calc-limit-iff-one-sided), both
one-sided limits of $f$ at ${-1}$ are the real number $\frac12$. By [part (a) of the proposition
that an infinite limit is not a real limit](#prop-calc-infinite-limit-no-real-limit), a one-sided
limit that is $\infty$ or $-\infty$ is no real number, so neither one-sided limit is $\infty$ or
$-\infty$, and the line $x = -1$ is not a vertical asymptote. The graph has a hole at
$\bigl({-1}, \frac12\bigr)$.

**Horizontal asymptotes.** $f = \frac{p}{q}$, where $p(x) = 2x^2 + 2x$ has degree $2$ and leading
coefficient $2$, and $q(x) = x^2 - 2x - 3$ has degree $2$ and leading coefficient $1$. By [part (c)
of the proposition on rational functions at infinity](#prop-calc-rational-at-infinity),
$\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = \frac21 = 2$ and $\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = 2$. So $y = 2$ is a
horizontal asymptote, by [the definition of a horizontal
asymptote](#def-calc-horizontal-asymptote). If $y = L$ is one, then $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$
or $\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L$, and in either case $L = 2$, by uniqueness, [property 1 of the
remark on limits at infinity](#rem-calc-limit-at-infinity-facts). So $y = 2$ is the only one.

::::{exercise} A root and a factor
:label: exr-calc-limits-review-root-and-factor
:class: tier-b

Find $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{\sqrt{x + 1} - 2}{x^2 - 9}$.

:::{admonition} Hint 1
:class: dropdown hint
Substituting $x = 3$ gives $\frac{0}{0}$. The numerator needs one technique, the denominator
another.
:::

:::{admonition} Hint 2
:class: dropdown hint
Multiply the numerator and the denominator by $\sqrt{x + 1} + 2$, and factor $x^2 - 9$.
:::

:::{admonition} Answer
:class: dropdown answer
$\frac{1}{24}$
:::
::::

::::{solution} exr-calc-limits-review-root-and-factor
:label: sol-calc-limits-review-root-and-factor
:class: dropdown

**Where it is defined.** Let $0 < \abs{x - 3} < 1$, so that $2 < x < 3$ or $3 < x < 4$, by
[part (e) of the proposition on distance inequalities](#prop-calc-abs-interval). Then
$x + 1 > 3 > 0$ (adding $1$, [property 3 of the order rules](#rem-calc-order-rules)), so
$\sqrt{x + 1}$ is defined ([the square-root remark](#rem-calc-square-roots)); and
$x^2 - 9 = (x - 3)(x + 3)$ is not $0$, by [part (a) of the sign rules](#prop-calc-sign-rules),
since $x \ne 3$ and $x \ne -3$. So the function is defined at every such $x$.

**Rationalise and cancel.** For such $x$, $\sqrt{x + 1} \ge 0$, so
$\sqrt{x + 1} + 2 \ge 2 > 0$ (adding $2$, property 3), and we may multiply the numerator and the
denominator by this non-zero number, which we call $s$. Since $\bigl(\sqrt{x + 1}\bigr)^2 = x + 1$
(the square-root remark), and cancelling the non-zero factor $x - 3$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sqrt{x + 1} - 2}{x^2 - 9}
  &= \frac{(x + 1) - 4}{(x^2 - 9)\, s} \\
  &= \frac{x - 3}{(x - 3)(x + 3)\, s} \\
  &= \frac{1}{(x + 3)\, s} .
\end{aligned}
$$

**The limit.** All the functions below are defined at every point of $(2, 4)$. By [part (a) of
direct substitution](#cor-calc-direct-substitution), $x + 1 \to 4$ and $x + 3 \to 6$ as
$x \to 3$. Since $4 > 0$, the square-root law, [part (f) of the limit laws](#thm-calc-limit-laws),
gives $\sqrt{x + 1} \to \sqrt{4} = 2$ (as $2 \ge 0$ and $2^2 = 4$). By part (a) and the sum law,
part (b), $s = \sqrt{x + 1} + 2 \to 4$; by the product law, part (c), the denominator $(x + 3)\,s$ approaches
$6 \cdot 4 = 24$; and by part (a) and the quotient law, part (d), since $24 \ne 0$, the quotient
approaches $\frac{1}{24}$. The original function agrees with it at every $x$ with
$0 < \abs{x - 3} < 1$, so by [the lemma on functions that agree except at a
point](#lem-calc-limit-agree-except-point), with $r = 1$,
$$
\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{\sqrt{x + 1} - 2}{x^2 - 9} = \frac{1}{24} .
$$

::::{exercise} An oscillation over a sine
:label: exr-calc-limits-review-oscillation-over-sine
:class: tier-b

Find $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^2 \sin(1/x)}{\sin x}$.

:::{admonition} Hint 1
:class: dropdown hint
The numerator and the denominator both approach $0$, so the quotient law does not apply. Write
the function as $\frac{x}{\sin x}$ times $x\sin(1/x)$.
:::

:::{admonition} Hint 2
:class: dropdown hint
For $x \sin(1/x)$, bound its distance to $0$ by $\abs{x}$ and squeeze. For $\frac{x}{\sin x}$, write
it as $1$ divided by $\frac{\sin x}{x}$.
:::

:::{admonition} Answer
:class: dropdown answer
$0$
:::
::::

::::{solution} exr-calc-limits-review-oscillation-over-sine
:label: sol-calc-limits-review-oscillation-over-sine
:class: dropdown

**Where it is defined.** Let $0 < \abs{x} < \frac{\pi}{2}$. If $0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$, then
$\cos x > 0$ by [property 8 of the point $P(t)$](#rem-calc-trig-functions-circle-properties). If
$-\frac{\pi}{2} < x < 0$, multiplying by ${-1}$ reverses both inequalities ([property 5(c) of the
order rules](#rem-calc-order-rules)), so $0 < -x < \frac{\pi}{2}$, and
$\cos x = \cos(-x) > 0$ by properties 4 and 8 of $P(t)$. By [part (c) of the lemma on sine and
cosine near 0](#lem-calc-sin-cos-near-zero), $\cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x}$, so
$\frac{\sin x}{x} > 0$ by transitivity ([property 2 of the order rules](#rem-calc-order-rules)).
In particular $\sin x \ne 0$. So the function $F(x) = \frac{x^2 \sin(1/x)}{\sin x}$ is defined at
every such $x$, and there
$$
F(x) = \frac{1}{\sin x / x} \cdot x\sin\Bigl(\frac{1}{x}\Bigr) .
$$
The right-hand side is defined at every $x$ with $0 < \abs{x} < \frac{\pi}{2}$, since
$\frac{\sin x}{x} \ne 0$ there.

**The first factor.** By [the theorem on the limit of $\frac{\sin x}{x}$](#thm-calc-sin-x-over-x),
$\frac{\sin x}{x} \to 1$, which is not $0$, and $1 \to 1$ by [part (a) of the limit
laws](#thm-calc-limit-laws). Both are defined at every $x \ne 0$, so the quotient law, part (d),
gives $\frac{1}{\sin x / x} \to \frac11 = 1$.

**The second factor.** Let $x \ne 0$. By [part (a) of the proposition on the bounds of sine and
cosine](#prop-calc-sin-bounded), with $t = \frac{1}{x}$, $-1 \le \sin(1/x) \le 1$, which says
$\abs{\sin(1/x)} \le 1$, by [part (b) of the proposition on distance
inequalities](#prop-calc-abs-interval) (centre $0$, radius $1$). By [property 4 of the absolute
value](#rem-calc-absolute-value-properties), and multiplying $\abs{\sin(1/x)} \le 1$ by the number
$\abs{x} \ge 0$, which gives only $\le$ ([property 5(b) of the order
rules](#rem-calc-order-rules)),
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Bigl\lvert x\sin\Bigl(\frac{1}{x}\Bigr) - 0\Bigr\rvert
  &= \abs{x}\,\Bigl\lvert\sin\Bigl(\frac{1}{x}\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert \\
  &\le \abs{x} .
\end{aligned}
$$
By [part (a) of the lemma on limits at 0](#lem-calc-sin-cos-limits-at-zero),
$\lim_{x \to 0} \abs{x} = 0$. By [the remark on squeezing the
distance](#rem-calc-squeeze-theorem-distance), with $a = 0$, $L = 0$, $r = 1$ and
$b(x) = \abs{x}$, $\lim_{x \to 0} x\sin(1/x) = 0$.

**Combine.** By the product law, part (c) of the limit laws, the right-hand side above approaches
$1 \cdot 0 = 0$. It equals $F(x)$ at every $x$ with $0 < \abs{x} < \frac{\pi}{2}$, so by [the
lemma on functions that agree except at a point](#lem-calc-limit-agree-except-point), with
$r = \frac{\pi}{2}$,
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x^2 \sin(1/x)}{\sin x} = 0 .
$$

::::{exercise} Salt in a tank
:label: exr-calc-limits-review-brine-tank
:class: tier-b applied

A tank holds $100$ litres of pure water. Brine with $30$ grams of salt per litre flows in at
$5$ litres per minute and mixes at once; the tank is large enough never to overflow. After $t$
minutes the tank holds $100 + 5t$ litres of liquid and $150t$ grams of salt, so the concentration
of salt is
$$
C(t) = \frac{150t}{100 + 5t}
$$
grams per litre, for $t \ge 0$. (a) Find $\displaystyle \lim_{t \to \infty} C(t)$, in grams per
litre. (b) Find the smallest number $N$ such that $C(t) > 29$ for every $t > N$.

:::{admonition} Hint 1
:class: dropdown hint
For (a), compare the degrees of the numerator and the denominator. For (b), multiply the
inequality $C(t) > 29$ by the denominator, and check its sign first.
:::

:::{admonition} Answer
:class: dropdown answer
(a) $30$ (b) $580$
:::
::::

::::{solution} exr-calc-limits-review-brine-tank
:label: sol-calc-limits-review-brine-tank
:class: dropdown

**(a)** $C = \frac{p}{q}$ with $p(t) = 150t$, of degree $1$ and leading coefficient $150$, and
$q(t) = 5t + 100$, of degree $1$ and leading coefficient $5$. By [part (c) of the proposition on
rational functions at infinity](#prop-calc-rational-at-infinity), the rational function
$\frac{p}{q}$ has the limit $\frac{150}{5} = 30$ as $t \to \infty$. It equals $C(t)$ at every
$t > 0$, so by [property 2 of the remark on limits at infinity](#rem-calc-limit-at-infinity-facts)
(only the tail matters), $\lim_{t \to \infty} C(t) = 30$, in grams per litre: in the long run the concentration approaches that of the
incoming brine.

**(b)** Let $t \ge 0$. Then $5t \ge 0$ ([property 5(b) of the order rules](#rem-calc-order-rules),
multiplying $t \ge 0$ by $5 \ge 0$), so $100 + 5t \ge 100 > 0$ (adding $100$, property 3). Multiplying
by the positive number $100 + 5t$ keeps a strict inequality, and multiplying back by its
reciprocal, which is positive (property 6), keeps it too (property 5(a)). So $C(t) > 29$ holds
exactly when
$$
\begin{aligned}
150t &> 29(100 + 5t) \\
  &= 2900 + 145t .
\end{aligned}
$$
Adding $-145t$ to both sides (property 3), and then multiplying by the positive number
$\frac15$, or back by $5$ (property 5(a)), this holds exactly when $5t > 2900$, that is, when
$t > 580$.

So every $t > 580$ has $C(t) > 29$, and $N = 580$ works. No smaller $N$ does: if $N < 580$, then
$t = 580$ satisfies $t > N$, but
$$
C(580) = \frac{87\,000}{3000} = 29,
$$
which is not greater than $29$. So the smallest $N$ is $580$ minutes, a little under ten hours.

In the language of [the definition of a limit at infinity](#def-calc-limit-at-infinity): for
$t \ge 0$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
C(t) - 30 &= \frac{150t - 30(100 + 5t)}{100 + 5t} \\
  &= \frac{-3000}{100 + 5t} < 0,
\end{aligned}
$$
because a negative number divided by a positive one is negative ([part (c) of the sign
rules](#prop-calc-sign-rules)). So $\abs{C(t) - 30} = 30 - C(t)$ ([the definition of the absolute
value](#def-calc-absolute-value)), which is less than $1$ exactly when $C(t) > 29$ (property 3).
So $580$ is the smallest threshold that wins the round $\eps = 1$, and the concentration stays
below $30$ throughout.

::::{exercise} A root over a line, far away and nearby
:label: exr-calc-limits-review-root-over-line
:class: tier-b

Let $f(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt{4x^2 + 1}}{x - 1}$. Find (a) $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x)$ and
(b) $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1^{-}} f(x)$. Write $\infty$ or $-\infty$ if the values grow without
bound.

:::{admonition} Hint 1
:class: dropdown hint
For (a): for $x < 0$, $\sqrt{x^2} = \abs{x} = -x$. Take $-x$ out of the root.
:::

:::{admonition} Hint 2
:class: dropdown hint
For (b): the numerator stays at least $1$. What does $\frac{1}{x - 1}$ do as $x \to 1^{-}$?
:::

:::{admonition} Answer
:class: dropdown answer
(a) ${-2}$ (b) $-\infty$
:::
::::

::::{solution} exr-calc-limits-review-root-over-line
:label: sol-calc-limits-review-root-over-line
:class: dropdown

**Where it is defined.** For every real $x$, $x^2 \ge 0$ (the "in particular" of [part (c) of the
sign rules](#prop-calc-sign-rules)), so $4x^2 \ge 0$ ([property 5(b) of the order
rules](#rem-calc-order-rules)) and $4x^2 + 1 \ge 1 > 0$ (adding $1$, property 3). So the root is
defined, and $f$ is defined at every $x \ne 1$. Moreover $1 \ge 0$, $\sqrt{4x^2 + 1} \ge 0$, and
$1^2 = 1 \le 4x^2 + 1 = \bigl(\sqrt{4x^2 + 1}\bigr)^2$, so $\sqrt{4x^2 + 1} \ge 1$ by the order part
of [the square-root remark](#rem-calc-square-roots) (and its uniqueness part for $=$).

**(a)** Let $x < 0$, so that $-x > 0$ (multiplying by ${-1}$, property 5(c)). Then $x^2 > 0$
(part (c) of the sign rules), so $\frac{1}{x^2} > 0$ (property 6) and $4 + \frac{1}{x^2} > 0$
(adding two positive numbers, property 4). The number $-x\sqrt{4 + \frac{1}{x^2}}$ is non-negative, with square
$x^2\bigl(4 + \frac{1}{x^2}\bigr) = 4x^2 + 1$. By the uniqueness in the square-root remark it is
$\sqrt{4x^2 + 1}$. Dividing the numerator and the denominator by the positive number $-x$, and
using $\frac{x - 1}{-x} = -1 + \frac{1}{x}$,
$$
f(x) = \frac{\sqrt{4 + \frac{1}{x^2}}}{-1 + \frac{1}{x}} \quad\text{for } x < 0 .
$$
As $x \to -\infty$, $\frac{1}{x} \to 0$ and $\frac{1}{x^2} \to 0$, by [part (a) of the proposition
on rational functions at infinity](#prop-calc-rational-at-infinity). By parts (a) and (b) of
[the limit laws at infinity](#thm-calc-limit-laws-at-infinity), as $x \to -\infty$,
$4 + \frac{1}{x^2} \to 4 > 0$, so the root approaches $\sqrt{4} = 2$ by part (f); and
$-1 + \frac{1}{x} \to -1 \ne 0$. By the quotient law, part (d), the right-hand side approaches
$\frac{2}{-1} = -2$. It equals $f(x)$ at every $x < 0$, so by [property 2 of the remark on limits
at infinity](#rem-calc-limit-at-infinity-facts), $\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = -2$.

**(b)** For $x \ne 1$, $f(x) = h(x)\,g(x)$ with $h(x) = \sqrt{4x^2 + 1}$ and
$g(x) = \frac{1}{x - 1}$. By [part (c) of the proposition on reciprocal
powers](#prop-calc-reciprocal-power-limits), with $a = 1$ and $n = 1$, which is odd,
$\lim_{x \to 1^{-}} g(x) = -\infty$. On $(0, 1)$ all three functions are defined and
$h(x) \ge 1$, as shown above. By the left-hand version of [part (a) of the proposition on a factor
that stays away from $0$](#prop-calc-infinite-limit-product), with $m = 1$ and the interval
$(0, 1)$, $\lim_{x \to 1^{-}} f(x) = -\infty$.

(As $x \to \infty$, the same steps with $x$ in place of $-x$ give the limit $2$: the graph has the
two horizontal asymptotes $y = -2$ and $y = 2$, and the vertical asymptote $x = 1$.)

::::{exercise} A positive limit over a square
:label: exr-calc-limits-review-positive-over-square
:class: tier-c rigor

Let $a$ be a real number, and let $f$ be a function that is defined at every point of an open
interval containing $a$, except possibly at $a$, with $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$, where $L > 0$.
(a) Prove that there is an $r > 0$ such that $f$ is defined and $f(x) > \frac{L}{2}$ at every $x$
with $0 < \abs{x - a} < r$. (b) Prove that
$$
\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{(x - a)^2} = \infty .
$$

:::{admonition} Hint 1
:class: dropdown hint
For (a), play one round of [the definition of the limit](#def-calc-limit). Which $\eps$ makes
$\abs{f(x) - L} < \eps$ force $f(x) > \frac{L}{2}$?
:::

:::{admonition} Hint 2
:class: dropdown hint
For (b), write $\frac{f(x)}{(x - a)^2} = f(x) \cdot \frac{1}{(x - a)^2}$, and treat each side of
$a$ on its own.
:::

:::{admonition} Answer
:class: dropdown answer manual
(a) The $\delta$ that wins the round $\eps = \frac{L}{2}$ is such an $r$. (b) On each side of $a$,
$f(x) \ge \frac{L}{2}$ by (a), and $\frac{1}{(x - a)^2} \to \infty$; a positive factor that stays
away from $0$ keeps the infinite limit.
:::
::::

::::{solution} exr-calc-limits-review-positive-over-square
:label: sol-calc-limits-review-positive-over-square
:class: dropdown

**(a)** Multiplying $L > 0$ by the positive number $\frac12$ keeps the inequality ([property 5(a)
of the order rules](#rem-calc-order-rules)), so $\eps = \frac{L}{2}$ is positive. By [the
definition of the limit](#def-calc-limit), there is a $\delta > 0$ such that $f$ is defined at
every $x$ with $0 < \abs{x - a} < \delta$, and $\abs{f(x) - L} < \frac{L}{2}$ there. Let
$r = \delta$, and let $0 < \abs{x - a} < r$. By [part (a) of the proposition on distance
inequalities](#prop-calc-abs-interval), with centre $L$ and radius $\frac{L}{2}$,
$$
L - \frac{L}{2} < f(x) < L + \frac{L}{2},
$$
and the left-hand inequality says $f(x) > \frac{L}{2}$.

**(b)** Let $r$ be as in (a), and let $g(x) = \frac{1}{(x - a)^2}$ and
$F(x) = \frac{f(x)}{(x - a)^2}$ for $x \ne a$. By [part (e) of the proposition on distance
inequalities](#prop-calc-abs-interval), every $x$ in $(a, a + r)$ or in $(a - r, a)$ has
$0 < \abs{x - a} < r$, so by (a) $f$ is defined there and $f(x) > \frac{L}{2}$, hence
$f(x) \ge \frac{L}{2}$. At these $x$, $F(x) = f(x)\,g(x)$.

*From the right.* By [part (a) of the proposition on reciprocal
powers](#prop-calc-reciprocal-power-limits), with $n = 2$, $\lim_{x \to a^{+}} g(x) = \infty$. By
[part (a) of the proposition on a factor that stays away from $0$](#prop-calc-infinite-limit-product),
with $h = f$, $m = \frac{L}{2} > 0$ and the interval $(a, a + r)$,
$\lim_{x \to a^{+}} F(x) = \infty$.

*From the left.* By part (b) of the proposition on reciprocal powers, since $n = 2$ is even,
$\lim_{x \to a^{-}} g(x) = \infty$. The left-hand version of part (a) of the proposition on a
factor that stays away from $0$, with the interval $(a - r, a)$, gives
$\lim_{x \to a^{-}} F(x) = \infty$.

*Both sides.* $F$ is defined at every point of $(a - r, a)$ and of $(a, a + r)$, and both
one-sided limits are $\infty$, so $\lim_{x \to a} F(x) = \infty$ by [part (c) of the definition of
infinite limits](#def-calc-infinite-limit).

The hypothesis $L > 0$ is needed. With $L = 0$ the conclusion can fail: $f(x) = (x - a)^2$ has
the limit $0$ at $a$ ([part (a) of direct substitution](#cor-calc-direct-substitution)), and the
quotient is $1$ at every $x \ne a$, so its limit is $1$, by [part (a) of the limit
laws](#thm-calc-limit-laws) and [the lemma on functions that agree except at a
point](#lem-calc-limit-agree-except-point); and a function with the real limit $1$ does not tend
to $\infty$ ([the proposition that an infinite limit is not a real
limit](#prop-calc-infinite-limit-no-real-limit) and [the theorem on a limit and its
one-sided limits](#thm-calc-limit-iff-one-sided)).

::::{exercise} Two parameters under a root
:label: exr-calc-limits-review-root-parameters
:class: tier-c

There is exactly one pair of real numbers $a$ and $b$ such that
$$
\lim_{x \to \infty} \Bigl(\sqrt{x^2 + ax + 1} - bx\Bigr) = 3 .
$$
Find (a) $a$ and (b) $b$, and show that no other pair works.

:::{admonition} Hint 1
:class: dropdown hint
Suppose the limit is $3$. Divide the function by $x$: what is the limit of the quotient, computed
in two ways? This decides $b$.
:::

:::{admonition} Hint 2
:class: dropdown hint
With $b$ known, the expression is "$\infty - \infty$": multiply and divide by
$\sqrt{x^2 + ax + 1} + x$.
:::

:::{admonition} Answer
:class: dropdown answer
(a) $6$ (b) $1$
:::
::::

::::{solution} exr-calc-limits-review-root-parameters
:label: sol-calc-limits-review-root-parameters
:class: dropdown

Let $F(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + ax + 1} - bx$, and let $x > \abs{a}$.

**Where it is defined.** Since $\abs{a} \ge 0$ ([property 1 of the absolute
value](#rem-calc-absolute-value-properties)), $x > 0$ by transitivity ([property 2 of the order
rules](#rem-calc-order-rules)). By property 3 of the absolute value, $a \ge -\abs{a}$; adding this
to $x > \abs{a}$ ([property 4 of the order rules](#rem-calc-order-rules)) gives
$x + a > 0$. Multiplying by the positive number $x$ (property 5(a)), $x(x + a) > 0$, so
$x^2 + ax + 1 > 1 > 0$ (adding $1$, property 3). So $F$ is defined at every $x > \abs{a}$, and so
is
$$
1 + \frac{a}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2} = \frac{x^2 + ax + 1}{x^2},
$$
which is positive, as a quotient of two positive numbers ([part (c) of the sign
rules](#prop-calc-sign-rules)). The number $x\sqrt{1 + \frac{a}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}}$ is
non-negative, and its square is $x^2 + ax + 1$, so by the uniqueness in [the square-root
remark](#rem-calc-square-roots),
$$
\sqrt{x^2 + ax + 1} = x\sqrt{1 + \frac{a}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}} .
$$

**The root, after dividing by $x$.** By [part (a) of the proposition on rational functions at
infinity](#prop-calc-rational-at-infinity), $\frac{1}{x} \to 0$ and $\frac{1}{x^2} \to 0$ as
$x \to \infty$. By parts (a), (b) and (c) of [the limit laws at
infinity](#thm-calc-limit-laws-at-infinity), $1 + \frac{a}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2} \to 1$, and since
$1 > 0$, part (f) gives
$$
\sqrt{1 + \frac{a}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}} \to \sqrt{1} = 1 .
$$

**$b$ must be $1$.** Suppose that $\lim_{x \to \infty} F(x) = 3$. For $x > \abs{a}$, dividing by
$x > 0$,
$$
\frac{F(x)}{x} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{a}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}} - b .
$$
By the difference law, part (b), the right-hand side approaches $1 - b$. On the other hand,
$\frac{F(x)}{x} = F(x) \cdot \frac{1}{x}$ approaches $3 \cdot 0 = 0$, by the product law, part (c).
By uniqueness, [property 1 of the remark on limits at infinity](#rem-calc-limit-at-infinity-facts),
$1 - b = 0$, so $b = 1$.

**$a$ must be $6$.** With $b = 1$, and $x > \abs{a}$, the number $\sqrt{x^2 + ax + 1} + x$ is
positive, as the sum of a non-negative and a positive number. Multiplying and dividing by it,
using $\bigl(\sqrt{x^2 + ax + 1}\bigr)^2 = x^2 + ax + 1$ (the square-root remark), and then
dividing the numerator and the denominator by $x > 0$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
F(x) &= \frac{(x^2 + ax + 1) - x^2}{\sqrt{x^2 + ax + 1} + x} \\
  &= \frac{a + \frac{1}{x}}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{a}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}} + 1} .
\end{aligned}
$$
The numerator approaches $a$ (parts (a) and (b)), and the denominator $1 + 1 = 2 \ne 0$, so by the
quotient law, part (d), $F(x) \to \frac{a}{2}$. By uniqueness again, $\frac{a}{2} = 3$, so
$a = 6$.

**The pair works.** Conversely, for $a = 6$ and $b = 1$, the last step shows that
$\lim_{x \to \infty} F(x) = \frac{6}{2} = 3$. So $a = 6$ and $b = 1$ is a pair with the limit $3$,
and the steps above show that it is the only one: for large $x$ the graph of
$\sqrt{x^2 + 6x + 1}$ approaches the line $y = x + 3$.

## Chapter summary

- **The limit.** $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means that every tolerance $\eps > 0$ is met by a
  window $0 < \abs{x - a} < \delta$ ([the definition of the limit](#def-calc-limit)). The value
  $f(a)$ plays no part ([the value at $a$ does not matter](#rem-calc-limit-value-irrelevant)),
  and a limit is unique ([uniqueness of limits](#thm-calc-limit-unique)).
- **One side at a time.** [One-sided limits](#def-calc-one-sided-limit) use half-windows. The
  limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal ([a limit and its one-sided
  limits](#thm-calc-limit-iff-one-sided)); different one-sided limits mean a jump and no limit
  ([when the one-sided limits differ](#cor-calc-one-sided-limits-differ)).
- **Laws.** Limits respect sums, products, quotients with a non-zero limit below, powers and square
  roots ([the limit laws](#thm-calc-limit-laws)); for polynomials and rational functions with a
  non-zero denominator at $a$, the limit is the value ([direct
  substitution](#cor-calc-direct-substitution)).
- **The form $\frac{0}{0}$.** Factor, rationalise or simplify, then use [the lemma on functions
  that agree except at a point](#lem-calc-limit-agree-except-point): changing a function at $a$
  does not change its limit.
- **Squeezing.** A function trapped between two with the same limit has that limit ([the squeeze
  theorem](#thm-calc-squeeze)); it is enough to bound the distance $\abs{f(x) - L}$ by something
  that approaches $0$ ([squeezing the distance](#rem-calc-squeeze-theorem-distance)). With it,
  [$\frac{\sin x}{x} \to 1$](#thm-calc-sin-x-over-x) and
  [$\frac{1 - \cos x}{x} \to 0$](#cor-calc-one-minus-cos-over-x) as $x \to 0$.
- **Infinite limits.** [$f(x) \to \infty$](#def-calc-infinite-limit) means that $f(x)$ exceeds
  every height near $a$; such a limit is not a real limit ([an infinite limit is not a real
  limit](#prop-calc-infinite-limit-no-real-limit)). Signs decide the side: [reciprocal
  powers](#prop-calc-reciprocal-power-limits) and [a factor that stays away from
  $0$](#prop-calc-infinite-limit-product). They give [vertical
  asymptotes](#def-calc-vertical-asymptote).
- **Limits at infinity.** [$\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$](#def-calc-limit-at-infinity) uses a
  threshold $N$ in place of a window; the same [laws hold at
  infinity](#thm-calc-limit-laws-at-infinity), and the degrees decide the limit of a [rational
  function at infinity](#prop-calc-rational-at-infinity). Finite limits there give [horizontal
  asymptotes](#def-calc-horizontal-asymptote).
